# STEP 2: Review of "FactorJEPA on DENSEWORLD-115k: Can a World Model Predict Crowded, Chaotic South Asian Streets?" for the AAAI-27 Cog2Soc bridge

**Reviewer's summary.** The paper contributes DENSEWORLD-115k, 115,687 clips (276 hours) cut from 714 public YouTube videos of 22 Indian cities and released as metadata with a rebuild script, and FactorJEPA, a V-JEPA 2.1 predictor that composes each predicted embedding from layout, agent and interaction channels, anchored during training to automatic Grounding DINO and SAM 3 targets (Sec 2, Sec 3). Against seven adaptation methods at 2B and 1B scale on a 10k-clip set, and against LoRA and the frozen backbone on the full corpus, one fixed configuration lowers masked and causal prediction error and, at 115k, separates on four "predictive structure" diagnostics with paired BCa intervals, while losing on most temporal coherence diagnostics (Table 1, Figure 3). For Cog2Soc, the paper names negotiated, mixed-traffic street behaviour as its human phenomenon and proposes the learned interaction channel as a counterpart to hand-set social-force and agent-based rules (Sec 1, Sec 5). The evaluation is controlled and unusually candid for four pages, and the corpus is a real cross-cultural resource. But no result yet speaks about human behaviour itself: every number is an embedding error or a linear probe (Sec 6), the interaction channel is trained on a proximity and motion heuristic and never checked against people (Sec 2, Sec 6), and the cross-cultural claim rests on two density measures in Figure 2. It is a good discussion paper that is one behavioural analysis away from a strong one.

All page numbers refer to `v2_Cog2Soc/0_main.pdf` (pp. 1 to 4 text, p. 4 to 5 references).

## PART A. Acceptance probability

| Venue | Acceptance probability |
|---|---|
| AAAI-27 Cog2Soc bridge | **55%** |
| Typical AAAI-27 computer vision or embodied AI workshop | **75%** |

**Reason.** Bridges are inclusive and non-archival, and this paper follows the call's three required elements almost literally: Sec 1 has paragraphs headed "The human phenomenon", "Our approach" and "Connection across communities" (p. 1). Its evidence is far above the AAAI-26 calibration bar, where a paper with no quantitative results (3) and a paper built on one pair of videos (5) were accepted. What holds it back at Cog2Soc is relevance as judged by a behavioural or social-science reviewer: the phenomenon is described in prose but never measured, and the results speak to embedding prediction rather than to negotiation, yielding or norms (Sec 6: "Every diagnostic lives in the frozen V-JEPA embedding space or a linear probe on it"). With two reviews, I expect one positive ML-leaning reviewer and one borderline social-science reviewer, which makes the outcome close to a coin flip with a slight edge for acceptance. At a computer vision or embodied AI workshop, the dataset, the factorized predictor and the controlled comparison are the core contribution and the behavioural gap matters much less; the remaining risks there are the missing plain-predictor ablation and the clip-level split (Sec 6). One process note: the same work is a pending student abstract, under review at another non-archival workshop, and a public preprint; this is usually allowed for non-archival bridges, but the Cog2Soc dual-submission rule should be checked.

### C1. Human check of the automatic agents and interactions (planned 100 frames, 3 raters)
- **What it shows:** whether Grounding DINO and SAM 3 find road users in dense Indian scenes (precision and recall per agent class) and whether the automatic interaction edges match human judgments of who adjusts to whom. This answers the Sec 6 gap ("not yet checked against human judgements of who yields to whom") and gives the bridge its first behavioural number.
- **Design:** sample 100 anchor frames stratified by the ten scene types; show each rater the frame with the automatic boxes plus its 4 to 10 s clip (a still frame cannot show yielding); raters mark missed and false agents and, for up to three automatic edges per frame, answer "does one road user adjust to the other (yield, slow, swerve), and which one?". Report precision, recall, Fleiss κ, and the share of strong vs weak edges that raters judge as real interactions.
- **Needs:** no GPU; about 3 to 4 hours per rater plus 2 hours of analysis. Optional: about 1 GPU hour to read out FactorJEPA's interaction coordinates on the rated clips and correlate them with the human answers.
- **Where:** two sentences and one line of numbers at the end of Sec 2 after the pipeline sentence; replace the Sec 6 clause about human judgements with the result. Fits in the free space on p. 4.
- **Rubric gain:** label validity 3 to 6 (+3); bridge 5 to 6 (+1). If agreement is low, report it: the paper still gains, because the question is answered.

### C2. Where does crowded prediction get hard? Split the 1B·115k results by agent count and scene type
- **What it shows:** whether FactorJEPA's margin over LoRA and the frozen backbone grows with agents per clip (for example 0 to 2, 3 to 5, 6 to 10, more than 10) and by scene type, and the absolute error of each model per bin. Either outcome is a statement about crowds: "prediction error rises with density and factorization helps most (or not at all) in the densest scenes".
- **Needs:** saved per-clip test errors for the three 1B·115k models, plus agent counts from the automatic pipeline for the test clips that have targets (86,831 clips have targets, Sec 2). CPU only, about 2 to 3 hours. If per-clip errors were not saved, re-scoring the 23,106 test clips with three models is inference only, roughly 3 to 8 GPU hours.
- **Where:** replace Figure 2 (two full-width panels, top half of p. 3) with one column-width figure of two small panels: top, agents per clip for DENSEWORLD vs the reference per scene type (move pixel share to one sentence with the market and flyover numbers); bottom, the FactorJEPA margin with clustered intervals per agent-count bin.
- **Rubric gain:** behavioural insight 3 to 5 (+2); cross-cultural design 4 to 5 (+1).

### C3. Cross-cultural model test on the BDD100K and nuScenes reference
- **What it shows:** whether a video world model's prediction error is higher on DENSEWORLD than on reference clips of the same scene types, and whether DENSEWORLD adaptation (FactorJEPA and LoRA at 1B·115k) changes error on the reference. This turns Sec 2's "a behaviour model calibrated on the Western reference would meet denser scenes here" into a tested statement about models, not only about data.
- **Needs:** the reference clips already assembled for Figure 2; inference with the frozen backbone, LoRA and FactorJEPA at 1B, roughly 2 to 4 GPU hours for a few thousand clips; CPU bootstrap. State the caveat that absolute embedding errors differ across datasets, so compare per scene type and report each model's gap.
- **Where:** a small inline table (three models by two domains, future-frame L1 and causal-block L1) at the end of Sec 4 Findings, using the free space on p. 4, plus one sentence in Sec 5.
- **Rubric gain:** cross-cultural design 4 to 6 (+2); behavioural insight +0.5.

> We contribute DENSEWORLD-115k, 115,687 clips (276 hours) from 714 public YouTube videos of 22 Indian cities, released as reconstructible metadata, and FactorJEPA, which replaces V-JEPA's monolithic predictor with separate layout, agent, and interaction channels.
